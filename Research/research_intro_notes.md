# Research Methodology — Notes

## 1. What Is Research?

**Research** is the systematic process of creating new knowledge or insights that can be verified and shared.

### The Research Toolkit
- **Curiosity** — drives you to ask questions
- **Rigor** — test your answers, don't just assume them
- **Methodical iteration** — refine the process as you go

### Where to Begin
Start by asking a question, then cycle through:

**Building → Observing → Interpreting**

---

## 2. Types of Research

| Type | Primary Goal | Example Question Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Experimental** | Test cause and effect | Does X cause Y? |
| **Observational** | Describe a phenomenon | What are the characteristics of Z? |
| **Qualitative** | Understand experiences | How do people interpret event A? |
| **Computational** | Model complex systems | Can a simulation predict outcome B? |

---

## 3. What Makes a Question "Researchable"?

> A **researchable question** is one that can be answered through systematic collection and analysis of evidence.

**Qualities of a good research question — it should be:**
- Specific
- Cause-focused
- Answerable
- Complex (enough to be worth investigating)
- Measurable in its effect
- Clear about its subject
- Set in a controllable setting

---

## 4. The Hypothesis Triangle

Three connected steps that move you from question → testable hypothesis:

1. **The Question** — What is the relationship between X and Y?
2. **The Variables**
   - *Independent (X)* — what you change
   - *Dependent (Y)* — what you measure
3. **The Hypothesis** — State your predicted answer to the question

The triangle shape is deliberate: these three keep feeding back into each other — a shaky hypothesis usually means revisiting the question or the variables.

---

## 5. How to Read a Research Paper: The 3-Pass Method

Also called the "smart reading" technique — go shallow to deep instead of reading start-to-finish once:

1. **The Big Picture** — high-level overview: read in this order — title, abstract, summary/conclusions, then skim the introduction/theory — to grasp what the paper claims before diving into how it gets there
2. **Grasp the Content** —  dig into the main arguments, working through results and methology to see how the claims are actually supported
3. **The Critical Eye** — engage deeply: question assumptions, weigh the evidence
